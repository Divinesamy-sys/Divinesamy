# DeFi Explained: How Decentralized Finance Works

## Overview

**Decentralized finance (DeFi)** refers to financial applications and services built on blockchain networks.

Traditional financial services usually rely on banks, brokers, exchanges, or other centralized institutions to manage accounts and transactions.

DeFi applications use **smart contracts** to automate many of these functions.

Instead of opening an account with a traditional financial institution, users can interact with a DeFi application using a compatible crypto wallet.

A simplified DeFi workflow looks like this:

**User → Wallet → DeFi Application → Smart Contract → Blockchain**

DeFi can support activities such as:

* Lending
* Borrowing
* Exchanging tokens
* Providing liquidity
* Earning yield

## How Does DeFi Work?

DeFi applications are usually built using **smart contracts** deployed on a blockchain.

A smart contract is a program stored on a blockchain that automatically carries out instructions when specific conditions are met.

For example, imagine Person A deposits cryptocurrency into a DeFi lending protocol. The smart contract can record the deposit, calculate interest according to the protocol's rules, and allow other users to borrow available assets.

Users normally interact with smart contracts through a **decentralized application (dApp)**.

In simple terms, the dApp provides the interface, while the smart contract handles the underlying blockchain operations.

## Lending

DeFi lending allows users to supply crypto assets to a lending protocol.

The deposited assets can then become available for other users to borrow.

For example:

1. **Person A** deposits a supported cryptocurrency into a lending protocol.
2. The smart contract records Person A's deposit.
3. **Person B** wants to borrow cryptocurrency from the protocol.
4. Person B provides the required collateral.
5. The protocol allows Person B to borrow according to its rules.
6. Interest paid by borrowers may be distributed to users who supplied assets.

The exact process, interest rates, and rewards depend on the protocol.

In this way, DeFi can allow users to participate as both **lenders and borrowers without applying for a traditional bank loan**.

## Borrowing

DeFi borrowing allows users to obtain crypto assets through a blockchain-based lending protocol rather than a traditional bank.

Many DeFi lending systems use **overcollateralized loans**. This means that the borrower deposits more value as collateral than the value of the assets they want to borrow.

For example, Person A might deposit $1,500 worth of cryptocurrency as collateral and borrow $1,000 worth of another crypto asset.

If the value of Person A's collateral falls significantly, the protocol may automatically sell some or all of the collateral to help repay the loan.

This process is handled by smart contracts according to predefined rules rather than by a human loan officer.

## Decentralized Exchanges

A **decentralized exchange (DEX)** allows users to exchange crypto assets through blockchain-based protocols.

Unlike a traditional centralized exchange, users generally connect their own wallets and approve transactions themselves.

A typical DEX interaction might look like:

**Connect wallet → Select tokens → Enter amount → Review transaction → Approve/sign → Submit transaction → Receive tokens**

For example, Person A wants to exchange Token A for Token B. Person A connects a wallet to a DEX, selects the two tokens, reviews the expected amount and transaction fee, and then signs the transaction.

The exact process depends on the DEX and the blockchain being used.

## Liquidity Pools

Many DeFi applications use **liquidity pools**.

A liquidity pool is a collection of crypto assets locked in a smart contract. These assets can be used to support activities such as token swaps.

For example, imagine a liquidity pool containing Token A and Token B.

Person A deposits both tokens into the pool to provide liquidity. Other users can then trade between the two tokens using the available liquidity.

People who provide assets to a liquidity pool are called **liquidity providers (LPs)**.

Depending on the protocol, liquidity providers may receive a portion of the fees generated by transactions involving the pool.

However, providing liquidity also involves risks, including changes in token prices and potential losses associated with the pool's design.

## What Is Yield?

In DeFi, **yield** generally refers to the return generated from supplying assets to a protocol or participating in a DeFi activity.

Yield can come from sources such as:

* Lending interest
* Trading fees
* Protocol incentives
* Staking rewards
* Other protocol-specific mechanisms

For example, Person A deposits cryptocurrency into a DeFi lending protocol. If the protocol charges borrowers interest and distributes part of that interest to lenders, Person A may earn a return on the deposited assets.

Yield is not necessarily guaranteed.

A high advertised yield may result from temporary incentives, market conditions, or activities that involve significant risk. Users should therefore understand **where the yield comes from** rather than looking only at the advertised percentage.

## The Role of Smart Contracts

Smart contracts are a fundamental part of many DeFi applications.

They can automate actions such as:

* Accepting deposits
* Recording balances
* Processing swaps
* Managing collateral
* Calculating fees
* Distributing rewards
* Executing liquidations

For example, a lending smart contract can specify how much a user can borrow, how much collateral they need to provide, and what happens if the value of that collateral falls too far.

The smart contract then follows these programmed rules when the required conditions are met.

## Why Is DeFi Different From Traditional Finance?

The main difference is **how financial services are delivered and managed**.

### Traditional Finance

A typical traditional financial service may involve:

**Customer → Bank/Financial Institution → Internal Systems → Transaction**

The financial institution manages accounts, processes transactions, and controls access to its services.

For example, if Person A wants to borrow money from a bank, the bank may assess their application, determine the terms of the loan, and manage the loan through its internal systems.

### DeFi

A typical DeFi interaction may look like:

**User → Wallet → dApp → Smart Contract → Blockchain**

Instead of a bank managing the entire process, smart contracts and blockchain infrastructure perform many of the required functions.

For example, Person A can connect a wallet to a DeFi lending application and interact directly with the lending protocol's smart contracts.

However, decentralization does not mean that DeFi applications are automatically safe, anonymous, or risk-free.

## Risks of DeFi

DeFi can provide new ways to interact with financial services, but it also introduces several technical and financial risks.

### Smart Contract Risk

A smart contract may contain bugs or security vulnerabilities.

For example, if a flaw in a lending protocol's smart contract allows an attacker to manipulate the system, users interacting with that protocol could potentially lose assets.

### Market Risk

Crypto asset prices can change significantly.

For example, Person A may deposit a cryptocurrency when it is worth $2,000, but its value could later fall to $1,500. This change can affect the value of Person A's position or collateral.

### Liquidation Risk

Borrowers may have their collateral liquidated if its value falls below the required level.

For example, if Person A borrows against cryptocurrency and the value of that collateral drops significantly, the protocol may automatically sell part of the collateral.

### Liquidity Risk

Some markets may not have enough liquidity to execute a transaction efficiently.

For example, if Person A tries to trade a large amount of a token in a pool with limited liquidity, the trade may significantly affect the token's price.

### Oracle Risk

Some DeFi applications rely on **oracles** to obtain information from outside the blockchain, such as asset prices.

For example, a lending protocol may use an oracle to determine the current value of a user's collateral.

If the oracle provides incorrect or manipulated information, it could affect how the protocol operates.

### Phishing and Wallet Security

DeFi users are still responsible for protecting their wallets and reviewing transaction requests carefully.

For example, Person A may visit a fake DeFi website that looks similar to a legitimate application. If Person A connects their wallet and approves a malicious transaction, their assets could be at risk.

### Protocol Risk

A DeFi protocol may depend on several smart contracts, tokens, or other protocols.

For example, if one protocol depends on another protocol for pricing, liquidity, or collateral, a problem with the external protocol could affect the first protocol as well.

## A Simple DeFi Example

Imagine Person A wants to earn a return on a crypto asset.

A simplified process could look like this:

1. Person A connects their wallet to a DeFi application.
2. They choose a supported asset.
3. They review the protocol's terms, fees, and current rate.
4. They approve the required blockchain transaction.
5. They deposit the asset.
6. The smart contract records the position.
7. Person A monitors the position and its associated risks.

The important point is that the smart contract handles the relevant blockchain operations according to its programmed rules.

## Key Takeaways

* **DeFi** provides financial services through blockchain-based applications.
* **Smart contracts** automate many DeFi operations.
* **Lending** allows users to supply assets to protocols and potentially earn interest.
* **Borrowing** often requires users to provide collateral.
* **DEXs** allow users to exchange tokens through decentralized protocols.
* **Liquidity pools** provide assets that can support activities such as token swaps.
* **Yield** can come from lending interest, trading fees, incentives, or other mechanisms.
* DeFi involves **technical, market, liquidity, smart contract, and security risks**.
* Users should understand how a DeFi protocol works before depositing or borrowing assets.
